Battery Storage Field Guide

Solar and wind generate on nature's schedule, not ours. Batteries don't make energy; they move it in time, from cheap midday sun to the expensive evening. Follow the same town as Chapter 1 through a home, a grocery store and a small factory to see what's inside a battery, how long it lasts, how to size one and what it really costs.

How a battery cell charges and discharges

Every battery, from a phone to a grid container, is built from cells. Here is one LFP cell, the kind most home and grid batteries now use. Press Charge or Discharge and watch where the lithium goes.

State of charge25%
lithium ion (Li⁺), moves inside the cell electron (e⁻), moves through the wire

Charging

  1. The charger (rooftop solar or the grid) pulls electrons out of the cathode and pushes them through the wire into the anode.
  2. Lithium ions follow on the inside. They leave the iron phosphate, swim through the electrolyte and slip through the separator.
  3. They tuck in between the graphite layers of the anode. The cell is full when the graphite holds all the lithium it can.

Discharging

  1. Lithium would rather be back in the cathode, so as soon as there's a path, ions leave the graphite and cross back through the electrolyte.
  2. Electrons can't cross the separator, so they take the long way round, through your lamp, fridge or the grid. That flow is the electric current you use.
  3. Ions and electrons meet again in the cathode. The cell is empty when the graphite has given up its lithium.

Ions inside, electrons outside

The separator is the trick. It lets lithium ions through but blocks electrons, so every ion that crosses inside forces one electron to travel through the outside wire. Nothing is burned or used up: the same lithium shuttles back and forth thousands of times.

Why about 3.2 volts?

Charging pushes lithium "uphill" into the graphite, like pumping water up into a tank. The height of that hill sets the voltage: about 3.2 V for LFP and 3.6–3.7 V for NMC. A little energy turns into heat each way, which is why you get back roughly 85–90% of what you put in.

Why cells wear out

Each cycle, a little lithium gets trapped in a thin film that grows on the anode and in other side reactions. Less lithium shuttling back and forth means less capacity. Heat speeds this up, which is why cooling matters. Try it in Wear it out.

From one cell to a battery. One cell is small. Wiring cells in a chain (series) adds up their voltage; wiring them side by side (parallel) adds up their capacity. A home battery holds dozens of large cells; a grid container holds thousands. See how they stack up in the Diagram. Battery makers name the electrodes by what they do while discharging, so the cathode stays the "cathode" even while charging. LFP stands for lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO₄); the anode is graphite, written C₆.

Battery state of charge

Batteries earn money from the gap between cheap midday power and expensive evening power. The more batteries a grid builds, the smaller that gap gets. In California, evening peak prices fell by about a third from mid-2025 to mid-2026, and most batteries there now earn more from being available (resource adequacy) than from the price gap. That's also why grid batteries there are mostly 4 hours: long enough to cover the whole evening after the sun drops.

Incentives in 2026 as of October 2026

  • Homeowners buying outright get no federal credit. The 30% residential credit (Section 25D) ended December 31, 2025. Many websites still list it.
  • Businesses and leased or PPA home systems can still use the commercial credit (Section 48E). Storage that starts construction before 2033 remains eligible.
  • New foreign-sourcing (FEOC) limits apply to projects claiming the credit, and they tighten each year through 2030.
  • State and utility programs now matter more. Examples: Oregon's battery storage credit and California's SGIP, which pays per kWh. Check your own state and utility.
  • Tariffs on imported cells and packs can move US prices a lot; BNEF estimated a 60% tariff would raise US turnkey costs by about 60%. Check current status before relying on any figure here.

Safety: layers, and the record so far

Layers of protection

Battery systems are protected in layers, fastest first. The BMS trips in milliseconds when one cell drifts out of range. Thermal management keeps cells in their comfortable range. Gas and heat detection, explosion venting, suppression and spacing between units are there so that if a cell does go into thermal runaway, the failure stays in one unit.

The fire most people have heard of, at Moss Landing, California in January 2025, was in a large indoor facility of an older design. It's a big part of why outdoor, spaced, containerized LFP systems are now the norm. Industry groups point to the code framework and the improving record; community groups raise questions about siting, emergency response, and smoke and runoff. Both are part of how projects get approved.

Standards to know by name: UL 9540 (system safety listing) · UL 9540A (thermal-runaway fire test) · NFPA 855 (installation code) · UL 1973 (stationary batteries) · IEEE 1547 (grid interconnection, from Chapter 3)
